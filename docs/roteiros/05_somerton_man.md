### [COLD OPEN]

The first of December, 1948. Just after dawn, on a quiet beach south of Adelaide, Australia. A man is lying on the sand, his head resting against the sea wall. His legs are crossed. He is wearing a good suit, a knitted pullover, and polished shoes. He looks... like he fell asleep there.

He did not fall asleep. He was dead. And when the police went through his pockets, they found no wallet. No identity card. No name. Then they looked at his clothes... and realized that someone had carefully cut the labels out.

Months later, a pathologist found something else. A tiny, tightly rolled scrap of paper, hidden in a secret pocket sewn into his trousers. On it, two words, printed in an old-fashioned typeface. Two words in Persian. [pause] "Tamám Shud."

> VISUAL: Macro shot of a torn scrap of paper, slowly unrolling. Text overlay: "TAMÁM SHUD".

They mean "it is ended." Or "it is finished." So who was this man... who went to such lengths to disappear... and who seemed to know exactly how his story would end?

### [THE PROMISE]

For more than seventy years, this was one of the strangest unsolved cases in the world. A body without a name. A poem without an explanation. A secret code no one could break. A phone number that led to a woman who said she didn't know him. And whispers... of Cold War spies.

### [CHAPTER 1 — THE MAN ON THE SAND]

Somerton Park is a seaside suburb of Adelaide, the capital of South Australia. In 1948, it was a calm place. Families walked along the shore in the evenings. The air smelled of salt and seaweed.

> VISUAL: Archival PD photo of Adelaide beaches in the late 1940s, or an AI still of a 1940s seaside promenade at dusk.

On the evening of November thirtieth, several people walking near the water later told police they had seen a man lying on the sand, in roughly the same spot. One couple reportedly saw him stretch out his arm, then let it fall. They assumed he was drunk... or simply asleep. Nobody went over to check.

> VISUAL: AI still — silhouettes of a couple walking on the beach at dusk, seen from behind, a distant shape near the sea wall.

The next morning, around six-thirty, he was still there. In the same position. Two men out for an early swim — or by some accounts, a jockey and his companions exercising horses [VERIFY] — found him and called the police.

> VISUAL: Text overlay: "1 December 1948 — approx. 6:30 a.m. [VERIFY]". Seagulls on wet sand.

He was in his forties, fit, with broad shoulders and strong hands. He was clean-shaven. His clothes were expensive, and clean. His shoes were polished — strangely clean for someone who had supposedly been walking around a beach all day. And in a time when almost every man in Australia wore a hat... he had none.

> VISUAL: AI flat-lay of 1940s men's clothing on a dark table: a double-breasted jacket, a knitted pullover, polished brown shoes. No person.

There was a cigarette, unlit, resting on his collar. In his pockets: a used bus ticket. An unused second-class train ticket from Adelaide to Henley Beach. A comb. A packet of chewing gum. A box of matches. And a cigarette packet of one brand... holding cigarettes of another.

> VISUAL: AI still — items laid out like evidence: tickets, comb, gum, matches, cigarette packet, each with a small paper tag.

What there was not... was anything with a name on it.

> VISUAL: Slow fade on the evidence table, one empty space in the row.

The autopsy did not make things simpler. The pathologist found no sign of violence. No wound. No struggle. The man's heart was healthy. But several of his organs showed signs that pointed toward one possibility. Poison.

> VISUAL: Archival-style still of a 1940s laboratory — glass bottles, scales, a microscope. No body.

And yet, every test for poison came back negative. One leading expert later suggested that if this was poison, it must have been something rare. Something that would break down in the body and leave almost no trace [VERIFY: Prof. Cedric Stanton Hicks reportedly named digitalis or strophanthin as candidates].

> VISUAL: Text overlay: "Cause of death: UNKNOWN".

So the police had a dead man... a possible murder... no weapon, no motive, no suspect. And no idea who the victim was. They released his description to the papers. They sent his fingerprints across Australia, and then around the world. And, one after another... every lead collapsed. But the man on the beach had left something behind. And it had been sitting, all this time, just a few miles away.

### [CHAPTER 2 — THE SUITCASE AND THE SCRAP]

In January 1949, staff at Adelaide's main railway station found a brown suitcase in the cloakroom. It had been checked in on November thirtieth, 1948 — the day before the body was found. No one had ever come back for it.

> VISUAL: AI still — an old railway cloakroom, rows of luggage on wooden shelves, one brown suitcase slightly apart. Text overlay: "Adelaide Railway Station — January 1949".

Inside were clothes, a dressing gown, slippers, toiletries... and some unusual tools. A stencil brush. A table knife cut down to a short, sharp point. Scissors. The kind of kit, investigators thought, that someone might use to stencil cargo... on a ship, perhaps.

> VISUAL: AI flat-lay — open vintage suitcase, folded clothes, a stencil brush and scissors arranged neatly.

There was also a reel of orange waxed thread. It matched thread that had been used to repair a pocket in the dead man's trousers. And reportedly, that thread was not a brand sold in Australia at the time [VERIFY].

> VISUAL: Close-up of a spool of orange thread on dark wood.

And just like the clothes on the body... almost every label had been removed.

Almost. On a tie, police found the name "T. Keane." On a laundry bag, "Keane." On a vest, "Kean." For a moment, it looked like a breakthrough. Investigators searched for anyone named Keane who was missing. They found no one. And many came to believe that these were the only labels left behind... precisely because they led nowhere.

> VISUAL: Three AI close-ups of fabric with stitched or ink-stamped names: "T. KEANE", "KEANE", "KEAN".

Think about that for a moment. A man arrives in Adelaide. He checks his suitcase at the station. He removes the labels from his clothes. He goes to a beach. And the next morning, he's dead. Was he hiding from someone? Was someone hiding him? Or... was he simply making sure that no one would ever know who he was?

> VISUAL: AI still — a lone figure in silhouette walking away down a 1940s street at night, wearing no hat.

The inquest opened in June 1949. The coroner heard from doctors, detectives, and witnesses. And at the end... he could not even rule on how the man had died. The case was left open. But before the inquest was over, the pathologist went back to the clothes. One more time. And he found something nobody had noticed before.

> VISUAL: Archival-style shot of a courthouse exterior in Adelaide; then a pair of hands in white gloves turning out a trouser pocket.


Sewn into the waistband of the man's trousers was a tiny hidden pocket — a fob pocket. And inside it, rolled up very small, was a scrap of paper. It had been torn from the last page of a printed book. Just two words.

> VISUAL: Macro still of a trouser waistband with a small hidden pocket; the rolled scrap partly visible.

"Tamám Shud."

> VISUAL: Text overlay, centered, in an old serif typeface: "Tamám Shud".

Library staff identified the words. They come from the very end of a famous collection of Persian poetry: the *Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam*, in the English translation by Edward FitzGerald. In 1940s Australia, it was a popular book. Soldiers carried pocket editions to war.

> VISUAL: Archival PD illustration from an early 20th-century edition of the Rubaiyat; a soft-focus image of a small leather-bound book.

The *Rubaiyat* is a book about time running out. About drinking deeply from life... because life is short... and death comes for everyone. And the final words, on the final page, are simply... "Tamám Shud." It is ended.

> VISUAL: Slow pan over the pages of an old book, candlelight, dust in the air.

In the newspapers, this became the "Tamam Shud case." Often misspelled as "Taman Shud," because of an early printing error in the press [VERIFY]. Police made a public appeal. Somewhere out there, they said, there was a copy of the *Rubaiyat* with its last page torn out.

> VISUAL: Archival PD newspaper clipping, headline mentioning "Tamam Shud" or "Taman Shud" (from Trove).

And then, in July 1949... someone came forward with exactly that.

### [CHAPTER 3 — THE BOOK, THE CODE, AND THE NURSE]

A man walked into a police station in Adelaide with a book. He explained that he had found it in the back of his car, which had been parked in Glenelg — the suburb right next to Somerton — around the time the body appeared on the beach [VERIFY: some accounts say the book was found on the floor or back seat, and that it was his brother-in-law who found it]. He hadn't thought much of it. Until he read about the case.

> VISUAL: AI still — the interior of a late-1940s car, a small book lying on the back seat, streetlight through the window.

It was a copy of the *Rubaiyat*. A New Zealand edition, published by Whitcombe and Tombs. And the last page... had been torn out. The police compared the scrap from the dead man's pocket with the gap in the book. Under the microscope, they were reported to match.

> VISUAL: Close-up — a torn final page; the scrap held next to it with tweezers.

But that wasn't all. On the back cover of the book, faint, written in pencil, were five lines of capital letters. The second line had been crossed out. The letters seemed to make no words. No sentences. Just... fragments.

It looked like a code.

The police sent it to experts, including specialists in naval intelligence [VERIFY]. They could not break it. In the decades that followed, amateur codebreakers, students, and professional cryptographers all tried. One popular idea is that the letters are not a cipher at all, but the first letters of words in a sentence — a kind of private shorthand. But there are too few letters to prove anything. To this day, no one has produced a solution that most experts accept.

And there was one more thing written on the back of that book. A telephone number. [pause] When police traced it, it led to a house in Glenelg... just a short walk from the beach where the man was found [VERIFY: distance often given as roughly 400 metres].

The number belonged to a young nurse. For decades, the public knew her only by a pseudonym, "Jestyn." Her real name, published only after her death, was Jessica Thomson, known to friends as Jo.

It's important to be clear about this. Jo Thomson was never accused of any crime. She was a witness — someone whose number happened to be written in a book. And, according to police, she cooperated.

> VISUAL: Neutral shot — a quiet suburban street in Glenelg, 1940s style, at midday.

She told detectives that she did not know the dead man. But she said that during the war, while she was working in Sydney, she had given a copy of the *Rubaiyat* to an army officer. His name was Alfred Boxall.

> VISUAL: Archival PD photo of wartime Sydney; a book being handed across a table, hands only.

For a moment, everyone thought the mystery was solved. The dead man had to be Boxall. But then police found Alfred Boxall in Sydney. Alive. And he still had his copy of the *Rubaiyat*. With the last page... intact.

> VISUAL: Text overlay: "Alfred Boxall — found alive, 1949. His copy: undamaged."

Police later showed Jo Thomson a plaster cast that had been made of the dead man's head and shoulders. According to the detective who was there, her reaction seemed to unsettle her — he reportedly felt she was close to fainting [VERIFY]. But she maintained that she did not know him. And she asked police to keep her identity private. They did.

> VISUAL: AI still of a plaster bust in shadow, seen from behind, in a dim police office. No realistic face.

She lived quietly for the rest of her life, and died in 2007 without ever speaking publicly about the case [VERIFY]. In later years, some relatives and researchers raised questions about what she may have known. Those are questions — and speculation. Nothing more. She was never shown to have done anything wrong.

> VISUAL: Slow pan across an empty wooden bench on a seaside promenade.

So... a man with no name, a code no one could read, and the phone number of a woman who said she'd never met him. To many people, there was only one explanation that fit. And it came straight out of the Cold War.

### [CHAPTER 4 — SPIES AND SHADOWS]

To understand the spy theory, you have to remember where Australia was in 1948. The Second World War had just ended. The Cold War was beginning. And in the deserts of South Australia, the British and Australian governments had begun testing rockets and weapons at a place called Woomera.

> VISUAL: Archival PD photo of Woomera rocket range or 1940s missile testing; a map of South Australia with Woomera and Adelaide marked.

At the same time, Western intelligence was secretly decoding Soviet messages that reportedly suggested information was leaking from Australian government circles [VERIFY: Venona decrypts, late 1940s]. That pressure contributed to the creation of Australia's domestic intelligence agency, ASIO, in 1949.

> VISUAL: AI still — a 1940s typewriter, stacks of classified folders stamped "SECRET", a desk lamp.

Now look at the Somerton Man through that lens. No labels. No ID. A secret pocket. A coded message. Possibly a rare, untraceable poison. And a link to Alfred Boxall, who, according to some accounts, had worked during the war in a unit connected to intelligence [VERIFY].

> VISUAL: Split-screen montage — the scrap of paper, the pencil code, the cut label, the rotary phone.

To many, it sounded like a spy who had been silenced. Or a courier whose contact never showed up.

> VISUAL: AI still — a lone silhouette standing at the sea wall at night, the lights of Glenelg in the distance.

But here's the thing. There has never been solid evidence for any of it. No intelligence file has ever been made public that names him. The "code" might be espionage... or a shopping list, a betting note, a line of poetry jotted from memory. The missing labels might mean a secret mission... or just a private man who didn't want to be found.

> VISUAL: Text overlay: "THEORY ≠ EVIDENCE".

There were other theories, too. That he was a sailor, given the stencil kit. That he was a dancer or a long-distance runner, because doctors noted unusually developed calf muscles [VERIFY]. That he took his own life. That he died of natural causes, and the strange details were just... coincidence.

> VISUAL: Quick montage — a 1940s cargo ship at a dock; ballet shoes; running track; all as AI stills, no faces.

Each idea explained some of the clues. None explained all of them. And without a name, there was no way to test any of them.

> VISUAL: Slow fade to a cemetery gate.

On June fourteenth, 1949 [VERIFY], the unknown man was buried in Adelaide's West Terrace Cemetery. Before the burial, his body had been embalmed, and the plaster cast of his head and shoulders had been made — in case someone, one day, might recognize him. The headstone did not carry a name. It said he was the unknown man found at Somerton Beach.

> VISUAL: AI still — a simple 1940s headstone in soft afternoon light, inscription unreadable, a small bunch of flowers at its base.

And for years afterward, people noticed something strange. Every so often... someone left flowers on the grave [VERIFY]. No one knew who.

> VISUAL: Close-up of fresh flowers on weathered stone; wind moving the petals.

And so, the man without a name waited. Through the 1950s. Through the 60s, the 70s, the 80s. Decades passed. Detectives retired. Witnesses died. The case slowly became a legend. Until a scientist at the University of Adelaide decided to look at it in a completely different way.

### [CHAPTER 5 — SEVENTY YEARS OF STRANGERS]

Over the decades, many people were proposed as the Somerton Man. Missing husbands. Wandering workers. Soldiers who never came home. Families wrote to police, hoping — or fearing — that the man on the beach was someone they loved. One by one, those possibilities were ruled out. Some of the men turned up alive. Others simply didn't match.

> VISUAL: AI montage of old letters and envelopes addressed to "South Australia Police"; a stack of index cards.

Then, starting in the late 2000s, Professor Derek Abbott, an engineer at the University of Adelaide, began working on the case with his students. At first, they focused on the code. But Abbott became convinced that the real answer was not in the letters. It was in the man himself. His DNA.

> VISUAL: AI still — a university lab bench, laptop with statistical charts, a printed photo of the pencil code pinned to a corkboard.

The problem was getting it. The body was in a grave. And for years, requests to exhume it ran into legal and practical obstacles. So Abbott turned to something else. The plaster cast. When it was made in 1949, a few strands of the man's hair had become trapped in the plaster.

> VISUAL: Close-up AI still — the edge of a plaster surface with a few fine hairs embedded in it, under a magnifying lens.

Those hairs became the key. Abbott teamed up with Dr Colleen Fitzpatrick, a forensic genealogist in the United States, who had helped identify unknown people in other cold cases. Old hair, with no root, is extremely difficult to get usable DNA from. But by the late 2010s, the technology was finally catching up.

> VISUAL: AI still — DNA sequencing machine, glowing screens with genetic data; a world map with connection lines between Australia and the US.

Meanwhile, the pressure to exhume kept growing. And in 2021... South Australia's Attorney-General approved it. In May 2021, under a forensic tent at West Terrace Cemetery, the remains of the Somerton Man were exhumed — seventy-two years after he was buried [VERIFY: 19 May 2021].

> VISUAL: AI still — a white forensic tent in a cemetery at dawn, police tape, no people visible up close. Text overlay: "West Terrace Cemetery — May 2021 [VERIFY]".

The remains were handed to Forensic Science South Australia for testing. Officials said it could take time. Because the body had been embalmed... and because so many years had passed... getting a clean DNA profile would not be easy.

> VISUAL: Archival-style shot of a sealed evidence box being carried into a laboratory corridor.

But Abbott and Fitzpatrick were already working on their own line of evidence. From the hair in the plaster. And in July 2022, they announced that they had a name.

> VISUAL: Black screen. One line of white text slowly appearing, letter by letter: "July 2022 [VERIFY]".

### [CHAPTER 6 — A NAME]

[whispers] Carl Webb.

> VISUAL: Text overlay: "Carl 'Charles' Webb (b. 1905 [VERIFY])".

According to Abbott and Fitzpatrick, the man on the beach was Carl "Charles" Webb, born in Melbourne in 1905 [VERIFY]. An electrical engineer and instrument maker. When he died, he would have been forty-three years old.

> VISUAL: Archival PD photo of 1940s Melbourne streets — trams, shopfronts, people seen from a distance.

How did they find him? They used genetic genealogy — the same method that has cracked other decades-old cold cases. From the hair, they built a DNA profile. Then they compared it with the profiles of people who had voluntarily uploaded their DNA to public genealogy databases. They found distant relatives. And from those relatives, they built a family tree... going back generation by generation... until the branches pointed to one man who had seemingly vanished from the records around the late 1940s.

> VISUAL: Animated family tree branching across the screen, with most names blurred; one branch highlighted.

Webb's life, as researchers pieced it together, was quiet. He was reported to have been interested in betting on horse races, and in poetry [VERIFY]. He had separated from his wife around 1947, and after that, the records go silent [VERIFY]. His wife was reportedly living in Melbourne — though some accounts say she had moved to South Australia [VERIFY]. Nobody appears to have reported him missing.

> VISUAL: AI still — a racing form on a café table beside a small book of poems and a cup of tea. No person.

Researchers also pointed to a possible family connection to the name "Keane" — the same name found on the clothes in the suitcase [VERIFY: confirm the exact relationship reported by Abbott/Fitzpatrick before stating]. If confirmed, it would mean those labels were not a false trail after all. They may have been a clue... sitting in plain sight for seventy years.

> VISUAL: Return to the close-up of the "T. KEANE" label, now with a soft glow.

Here's where we need to be careful. This identification came from independent researchers. It made headlines around the world. Many experts found it convincing. But as of the most recent public information available for this script, South Australia Police and the State Coroner had not formally confirmed it [VERIFY: check for any official SA Police / Forensic Science SA / Coroner statement after July 2022]. Officially, the testing on the exhumed remains was a separate process — and the formal case would be decided by that process, not by headlines.

So... after all this time, we may finally know who he was. But knowing his name only opens the next set of doors.

> VISUAL: Slow push down a long, dim corridor with doors on either side.

Because think about everything we still don't know.

> VISUAL: The evidence table from Chapter 1 returns, lit by a single lamp.

Why was a Melbourne engineer in Adelaide, more than four hundred miles from home [VERIFY: approx. 650 km / 400 miles]? Why did he check a suitcase at the station and never come back for it? Why did he — or someone else — remove the labels from his clothes?

> VISUAL: Map animation — a line from Melbourne to Adelaide across south-eastern Australia.

Why did he carry two words torn from a book of poetry, hidden in a secret pocket? And whose copy of that book was found in a car in Glenelg, with a code and a nurse's phone number on the back?

> VISUAL: The scrap of paper and the book, side by side; the pencil letters faintly visible.

The code has still not been solved. Not by navy cryptographers in 1949. Not by computer analysis decades later. Not by the thousands of people around the world who have tried. The letters are still there, in faint pencil... waiting.

> VISUAL: Extreme close-up of the pencil code; slow focus pull.

And the question that started it all — how did he die? — still has no confirmed answer. No poison was ever found. No injury. No clear natural cause. The coroner in 1949 could not say. And even with a name, that piece of the story may be lost for good.

> VISUAL: Text overlay: "Cause of death: STILL UNKNOWN".

And maybe that's the strangest irony of all. For seventy years, the world called him the Unknown Man. Now he may have a name, a birthplace, a family, a profession. And yet, the moment that mattered most — the last night of his life, on that beach — is as unknown as ever.

### [ENDING]

Somewhere in the decades between 1948 and today, the Somerton Man stopped being just a case. He became a story people told. A puzzle. A ghost story. A spy thriller. But he was a real person. Someone's son. Someone's husband. Someone who, for reasons we may never understand, ended up alone on a beach on a summer evening, watching the sea.

> VISUAL: Slow pan along the shoreline at dusk; distant silhouettes of people walking, never close.

Science has done something remarkable here. It gave a nameless man a name. But it can't tell us what he was thinking. It can't tell us who, if anyone, he was waiting for. And it can't read those pencil letters on the back of a book.

The last words he carried were from the last page of a poem about how quickly life passes. Two words. Chosen by him... or for him.

[pause] "Tamám Shud."

It is ended. [whispers] But for the people still trying to understand what happened on that beach... it never really has.
