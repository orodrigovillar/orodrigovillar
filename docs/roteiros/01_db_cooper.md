### [CHAPTER 1 — THE NOTE]

Portland International Airport. Wednesday afternoon. The busiest travel day of the year. A man walks up to the Northwest Orient counter and buys a one-way ticket to Seattle. He pays in cash, about twenty dollars [VERIFY: $18.52]. He gives his name as Dan Cooper.

Witnesses would later describe him the same way. Mid-forties. Around six feet tall. A dark suit, a narrow black tie, a raincoat, and a black briefcase. Quiet. Polite. The kind of man no one remembers.

Flight 305 was a short hop. About thirty minutes in the air. A Boeing 727, with thirty-six passengers [VERIFY] and a crew of six. Cooper took a seat near the back, in row eighteen [VERIFY: 18C vs 18E]. He ordered a bourbon and soda. He lit a cigarette, which in 1971 was completely normal. The plane pushed back from the gate a little before three in the afternoon.

Shortly after takeoff, Cooper handed a folded note to the flight attendant sitting closest to him, Florence Schaffner. She assumed what so many flight attendants of that era assumed. That a lonely businessman was giving her his phone number. She slipped it into her pocket without reading it.

Schaffner carried the message to the cockpit. Captain William Scott radioed the company. And the president of Northwest Orient, Donald Nyrop, made a quick decision. Pay him. Give him whatever he wants.

### [CHAPTER 2 — TEN THOUSAND TWENTIES]

On the ground in Seattle, the FBI went to work. Agents and bank officials gathered ten thousand twenty-dollar bills. And before handing them over, they did something that would matter for decades. They photographed every single bill on microfilm, recording all ten thousand serial numbers [VERIFY: method and timing].

> VISUAL: AI still — stacks of 1960s-series twenty-dollar bills under a camera lamp; text overlay "10,000 serial numbers recorded".

Parachutes were harder. The first ones offered came from a nearby air base, but Cooper reportedly refused military chutes. He wanted civilian ones. So the authorities found them at a local skydiving school. Two main chutes, and two reserves. One of those reserves, it would later turn out, was a training dummy, sewn shut. It could never have opened [VERIFY].

> VISUAL: AI still — four parachute packs laid out on a concrete hangar floor, one visibly different.

For roughly two hours, Flight 305 circled. Then, at about twenty minutes to six in the evening [VERIFY: 5:39 PM landing], the plane touched down at Seattle-Tacoma International Airport. Cooper ordered it parked in a remote, well-lit corner of the tarmac. He ordered the cabin lights dimmed, so that snipers outside couldn't see him.

> VISUAL: Archival-style still — Sea-Tac tarmac at night, floodlit, a 727 parked alone.

A Northwest employee walked out to the plane with a bank bag full of cash, and the parachutes. Flight attendant Tina Mucklow carried them inside. Only then did Cooper let the passengers go. All thirty-six walked down the front stairs. He also released Florence Schaffner and the senior flight attendant, Alice Hancock. But he kept four people on board. The two pilots. The flight engineer. And Tina Mucklow.

> VISUAL: AI still — a canvas bank bag on a cabin floor, cash corner visible; passengers as distant silhouettes descending stairs.

Mucklow would spend more time with Cooper than anyone else. By her accounts, he was calm, and not unkind. He never raised his voice. But he was in total control.

And then came his instructions for the second flight. They were the strangest part of the entire hijacking.

Fly toward Mexico City. Stay below ten thousand feet. Keep the speed as low as possible without stalling, around a hundred and fifty knots [VERIFY]. Landing gear down. Wing flaps lowered to fifteen degrees. Cabin unpressurized. And take off... with the rear stairs open.

The pilots objected that they couldn't take off with the stairway down. Cooper let them close it for takeoff. He would open it himself. They negotiated a fuel stop in Reno, Nevada, because the plane could not reach Mexico in that configuration.

Investigators would focus on one detail again and again. The Boeing 727 had a rear airstair built into its tail. And unlike almost any other airliner, that stair could be lowered in flight. Very few people outside the aviation world knew that. Cooper knew. And Cooper knew how slow, and how low, a man would need the plane to be... to survive stepping off it.

At around seven-forty that night, Flight 305 took off again, heading south into the storm. Cooper told Mucklow to go to the cockpit and stay there. The last time she saw him, he was standing in the cabin, tying something around his waist. Probably the money bag.

She closed the curtain. And she was the last person known to see Dan Cooper alive.

### [CHAPTER 3 — INTO THE DARK]

A few minutes after eight, a warning light in the cockpit showed the aft stairs had been opened. Over the intercom, the crew asked if he needed anything. According to their accounts, Cooper answered with a single word. No.

> VISUAL: AI still — a cockpit panel with one amber warning light glowing; text overlay "AFT STAIR".

Then, at about eight-thirteen, the crew felt it. The nose dipped, and the tail jumped upward, as if a weight had suddenly come off the end of the stairs. The cabin pressure shifted. And after that... silence. No more contact from the back.

The crew kept flying, all the way to Reno. When the plane landed, a little after ten-fifteen [VERIFY], agents surrounded it. The rear stairs were still hanging down, scraping toward the runway.

Inside, the cabin was empty. The money was gone. Cooper was gone. He had left behind only a few things. Two of the four parachutes, one of them the sewn-shut dummy, its cords cut away. Back in Portland, in the first hours of the investigation, the FBI questioned a local man whose name happened to be D.B. Cooper. He was quickly cleared. He had nothing to do with it. Cooper had shown the world a method. Ask for money. Ask for parachutes. Jump. In 1972 alone, there were more than a dozen copycat hijackings in the United States using the same basic plan [VERIFY: commonly cited as ~15].

The airlines and the government had seen enough. Boeing engineered a small mechanical device for the 727's tail. Years passed. The leads went cold. Many agents became convinced Cooper had died that night, in the trees or in the water. But the money had never surfaced. Not one bill. Until a family went on a picnic.

### [CHAPTER 4 — THE MONEY IN THE SAND]

February 10th, 1980 [VERIFY]. The Columbia River, a few miles downstream from Vancouver, Washington, on a sandy beach called Tena Bar. The Ingram family is spending a Sunday afternoon by the water. Their eight-year-old son, Brian, is raking the sand, getting ready to build a campfire.

Just a few inches below the surface, his hands hit something. Bundles of paper. Rotting. Fragile. Held together with rubber bands. He pulled them out. It was money. Twenty-dollar bills.

There were three packets, about five thousand eight hundred dollars in all [VERIFY: $5,800, 294 bills]. The family turned them in. And when the FBI checked the serial numbers against the microfilm from 1971... they matched. Nine years after the jump, part of D.B. Cooper's money had come out of the ground.

It should have solved everything. Instead, it made the case even stranger.

Tena Bar is not where the FBI expected the money to be. It is miles south of the original drop zone, along a different river system entirely [VERIFY]. How did it get there?

Maybe Cooper's flight path had been miscalculated. Maybe the money washed down a tributary into the Columbia. Maybe it was carried there by dredging. And there was another detail that bothered investigators. The bills were still bundled, with rubber bands. Money tumbling for years in a river should have scattered. Some people have suggested someone buried it there on purpose. Or that an animal dragged it. No theory has ever explained it completely.

### [CHAPTER 5 — THE TIE AND THE SUSPECTS]

For decades, the most intimate thing Cooper left behind sat in an FBI evidence box. The black clip-on tie. Investigators believed it came from J.C. Penney [VERIFY]. In the early 2000s, the FBI extracted a partial DNA profile from it [VERIFY: 2001]. But a tie can carry DNA from anyone who ever touched it, and the profile has never been matched to a known suspect.

> VISUAL: AI still — a black clip-on tie inside a clear evidence bag on a gray table, single overhead light.

Then, around 2009, the FBI allowed a team of independent scientists, who called themselves the Citizen Sleuths, to study material from the tie [VERIFY]. Using an electron microscope, they found something unexpected. Tens of thousands of microscopic particles [VERIFY: often cited as ~100,000].

> VISUAL: Electron-microscope style image (AI, abstract) — tiny metallic particles on woven fibers.

Among them were rare elements. Cerium. Strontium. And particles of pure titanium [VERIFY]. In 1971, pure titanium was used almost only in specialized industries. Aerospace. Chemical manufacturing.

> VISUAL: Text overlays appearing over particle image — "CERIUM", "STRONTIUM", "PURE TITANIUM".

The team suggested Cooper may have worked in, or around, a facility that handled these materials. Possibly connected to the aerospace boom in the Pacific Northwest. Boeing, the company that built the very plane he jumped from, was based in the Seattle area [VERIFY: researchers' specific hypotheses].

> VISUAL: AI still — a 1970s industrial plant at dusk, smokestacks, no people.

It's a fascinating clue. But a tie can be bought secondhand, or borrowed. And thousands of people worked in those industries. The particles narrowed the picture... without giving it a face.

And still, over the years, people have come forward with faces. Names. Confessions from relatives. Old photographs. The FBI has said it looked at more than eight hundred persons of interest [VERIFY]. So let's look at a few of the names that come up most often. And let's be clear from the start: none of these men was ever charged with this crime.

The first name, inevitably, was Richard McCoy. The man who copied the hijacking in Utah, just five months later. Some investigators and authors became convinced he was Cooper himself, and that Utah was simply his second attempt [VERIFY: Rhodes & Calame book]. He was a former Army paratrooper and helicopter pilot. He fit the profile of someone who knew how to jump.

But the FBI ultimately did not believe McCoy was Cooper. He was considerably younger than the man witnesses described, and he reportedly did not match the descriptions given by the Flight 305 crew [VERIFY]. And there are reports placing him with his family around Thanksgiving of 1971 [VERIFY]. McCoy died in 1974. If he knew anything, he never said.

The second name is Kenneth Christiansen. He had been an Army paratrooper in the 1940s, and later worked for Northwest Orient itself, as a flight attendant and purser [VERIFY]. After he died in 1994, his brother reportedly came forward with suspicions, and a 2007 magazine investigation brought his name to national attention [VERIFY: New York magazine, Geoffrey Gray]. Rackstraw denied being D.B. Cooper. He was never charged with the hijacking. And the FBI did not reopen the case against him. He died in 2019 [VERIFY]. Whatever the truth, it is not something we can settle here, and it would be wrong to pretend otherwise.

These are only three names among hundreds. Every few years, someone announces the mystery is solved. And every time, the case stays exactly where it was.

### [CHAPTER 6 — WHY NO ONE KNOWS]

Ralph Himmelsbach, the FBI agent who spent much of his career on this case, came to believe Cooper probably did not survive [VERIFY: Himmelsbach's view as stated in his book and interviews]. And the arguments for that are serious.

> VISUAL: Archival PD — FBI field office exterior, 1970s.

Cooper jumped at night, into a storm, wearing a business suit and loafers [VERIFY]. The temperature at that altitude was well below freezing, with wind and rain hitting him at high speed. He jumped over dense forest, with no ground crew, no lights, no landmarks. And he chose an older main parachute, apparently not the most modern one available [VERIFY]. For the reserve, he took... the dummy. The one sewn shut. To many investigators, that sounded like a man who knew less about parachutes than he wanted the crew to believe.

> VISUAL: AI still — a pair of leather loafers on wet moss under ferns, no body, purely symbolic.

But there's another side. Cooper knew the 727's rear stairs could open in flight. He knew the right speed, the right altitude, the right flap setting. He stayed calm for hours. He thought about snipers, fuel, and flight time. Whoever he was, he planned this carefully. And a careful man might also plan how to disappear afterward.

> VISUAL: Animated recap diagram — speed, altitude, flaps, aft stairs, each element ticking into place.

Then there is the money. If Cooper died in the woods, why has no one ever found his parachute, his body, or the bag? In 1978, a hunter did find a placard from a 727 aft stairway in the forest of southwest Washington, and investigators believed it likely came from Flight 305 [VERIFY]. But it led nowhere. And if he survived... why did only those few bundles at Tena Bar ever turn up?

> VISUAL: AI still — a weathered metal instruction placard lying in leaf litter, text not legible.

On July 12th, 2016 [VERIFY], the FBI announced it was ending its active investigation into the hijacking, after forty-five years. The Bureau said it would redirect resources to other priorities. The evidence would be preserved. And if someone ever came forward with physical evidence, specifically the parachutes or the money, they were still asking to hear about it [VERIFY: wording of the FBI statement].

> VISUAL: Text overlay — "JULY 2016: FBI ENDS ACTIVE INVESTIGATION".

So what do we actually know? We know a man calling himself Dan Cooper hijacked Northwest Orient Flight 305 on November 24th, 1971. We know he collected two hundred thousand dollars and jumped into the dark somewhere over southwest Washington. We know about five thousand eight hundred dollars of that money was found on Tena Bar in 1980. And we know that, despite one of the largest investigations in FBI history, no one has ever been charged.

> VISUAL: Slow montage recap — the note, the bank bag, the tie, the riverbank, the empty seat.

What we don't know is everything else. His real name. Where he came from. Whether he landed in the trees, in the water, or on his feet. Whether he went home that Thanksgiving... or never went home at all.
